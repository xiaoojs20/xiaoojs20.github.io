At the start of 2026, I set myself two goals: to bring about a personal Meiji Restoration within three years, and a family reform and opening-up within five.

These concepts do not map exactly onto their historical political and economic counterparts. I am borrowing from historical experience to think about how a person completes their own modernization, and how one builds a more modern family.

## A personal Meiji Restoration: a revolution in capability

**The core of the Meiji Restoration is a revolution in capability.**

In traditional society, the signals that charted a life's progress came mostly from the outside. Exams, schooling, job hunting, marriage — society and the education system supplied a fairly clear standard of evaluation. We only had to optimize within a given objective function to earn steady, stage-by-stage feedback. But in reality, many of life's questions have no standard answer, and even the objective function itself has to be defined by ourselves.

The first step in personal modernization is to establish your own central government: raise your level of understanding, form independent judgments, learn to make decisions, and learn to manage yourself.

Next comes personal infrastructure — technical skills at work, investment knowledge and financial accounts, legal identity and household registration, connections, or simply being lean and muscular. Before railways are built, even an ambitious country cannot develop real productive capacity. In the same way, without enough infrastructure, even the grandest values are difficult to put into practice.

At the same time, one needs the **spirit of the Iwakura Mission**: acknowledge how much of the world beyond us we do not know, and then actively go and see it. Stay long enough in one city, one profession, one social circle, and it is easy to mistake your local experience for the universal laws of how the world works. Many beliefs we hold most firmly may not be the result of independent thought at all, but the shared product of upbringing, education, and the people around us.

This is one reason I love to travel. Beyond natural scenery, you get to observe how people in different places live, and how they understand career, wealth, family and happiness. You meet different industries and different ages of people, and see what possibilities exist outside the path you know. Sometimes, encountering a way of life you had never imagined is more valuable than acquiring a specific piece of knowledge. It changes not only how we understand the world, but how we imagine our own future.

**Build our knowledge inwardly, and explore this blue planet outwardly.**

## A family reform and opening-up: an institutional revolution

**The core of reform and opening-up is an institutional revolution.**

One direction of reform is to reduce the excessive concentration and personalization of power, and to strengthen collective leadership, shared responsibility, and institutional governance.

Traditional family life depends to a large extent on the authority of elders, ethical obligation, and long-standing convention. In an era of scarce material resources and incomplete social security, this tightly knit structure had its rationale. The family was also an individual's main buffer against risk from outside.

But as family members diverge in education, economic capacity, place of residence and values, the mechanisms that once went without saying need to be reopened for discussion. The family reform I have in mind is a move away from over-concentrated and personalized power, toward transparent, equal, consultative, divided and open ways of operating.

How family assets are allocated, how members share responsibility, how a couple balances career and children, how parents are cared for in old age, how major disagreements are settled. None of these should lose the possibility of being discussed simply because that is how things have always been.

Affection cannot replace every institutional arrangement, nor should institutional arrangements replace affection. Talking through responsibility, interests and boundaries in advance is precisely how we reduce future conflict, and keep years of accumulated feeling from being consumed by problems that could have been handled well.

And openness means allowing family members to live different lives. Parents may have their own later years; children may choose their own careers, cities and partners; a couple may each keep their own friends, interests, careers and inner worlds. Family members need not share identical values, nor take part in every part of each other's lives.

I want family to be the safety net from which each person explores the outside world, not the reason they do not. Rather than requiring everyone to follow one life, it can acknowledge difference and find a sustainable way to cooperate over the long term.

## An age when old answers expire

**Our generation's personal modernization happens just as the broader economic tailwinds weaken and the external order grows more uncertain.**

For ordinary people, many assumptions that could once be taken for granted now need to be proved again. Can a degree reliably lead to a career? Can a job last ten years? Does buying a home necessarily build wealth? Will an industry's core skills today still be scarce five years from now? Is a city worth twenty years of one's life?

The future may well be an age in which old answers gradually lose their force while new ones have yet to form. We have to build our own knowledge system, because schooling cannot cover everything we will need to know in life. We have to cultivate our own career capital, because no organization can promise permanent security. We have to build our own financial system, because labour income is ultimately bounded by our time and energy. We have to manage our health proactively, because many health problems show no sign in youth. And we have to keep extending our social network, because some of life's most important opportunities come from beyond our existing circle.

The common value of all these things is that they add **life's optionality**.

## Economic independence and family relationships

This is why I care so much about economic independence.

While a person still depends on family for housing, income and social resources, their freedom is bound to be limited. Many generational conflicts that look like clashes of values are also questions of economic dependence and decision-making power.

For me, it is better to build the ability to live independently than to spend years persuading my parents to accept my values. Once I can bear the financial cost and real consequences of a decision, many questions that once required repeated argument gain a more equal footing for discussion.

Economic independence is not, of course, a way of gaining power over my parents. Even if one day my income and wealth exceed what they accumulated over a lifetime, that gives me no right to decide how they should live.

A family's reform and opening-up is not simply a transfer of power from one generation to the next, but a gradual expansion of each person's ability to decide their own life.

A relationship in which people remain willing to support one another after becoming independent, and remain respectful despite disagreement, may be more durable than one built on dependence and obedience.

## Witnesses to a life

Modernizing yourself does not mean becoming someone who needs no one.

People often say that good memories compound too. A journey in your youth, an experience worth remembering, a group of friends known for many years — these are recalled again and again over a long life, and take on new meaning. Some things, if endlessly postponed, cannot be recovered later even with greater material means.

I do not want to wait until my personal modernization is complete before I start living seriously. A personal Meiji Restoration never really reaches completion. We can be imperfect, remain uncertain and keep rebuilding, but we should at least learn to take responsibility for our unfinished selves, rather than making a partner carry the cost of our disorder.

A good partnership is, at its core, mutual growth rather than mutual rescue.

I hope that when two people come together, both lives grow wider rather than contracting into the household. Each continues to learn, work, meet new people and explore new fields, while bringing what they learn from the outside back into a shared life.

On their own, a person has one set of experiences, one perspective, one life trajectory. Together, two people can share memories, exchange judgments, correct each other, and gain more security and possibility than either had alone.

Life is finite. However hard we try, no one can live every possible life. One of the beauties of intimacy is that another person's experiences become partly our own.

More importantly, over a long enough stretch of time, a partner becomes a witness to your life: someone who knows what you believed when young, why you changed, and has seen your ambition, exhaustion, failures and fresh starts.

Look back decades later, and the ordinary days that seemed insignificant at the time may turn out to be the most precious memories you share.

## The purpose of development

Development itself, of course, is not the final goal of life.

A country's development cannot be measured only by growth in output, and neither can a person's be measured only by wealth, knowledge and capability. If, in the course of accumulating these things, we lose the ability to feel life — with no time to experience the world and no energy for the people who matter — then the meaning of development needs to be reconsidered.

I want to keep accumulating wealth, but not to spend a lifetime thinking about how to earn more. I want to keep learning, but also to have time for knowledge with no immediate economic value. I want a healthy body not only to sustain longer productivity, but so that in my sixties and seventies I still have the strength to see the world.

The economic freedom I seek is not an end to work, but the ability to decide more freely what is worth giving one's life to.

Much of life's beauty needs no achievement to justify it. Such as this afternoon, basking in the sun by Erhai Lake.

No task to finish, no value to prove, no need to calculate how much return the afternoon could generate. Just watching the water, feeling the sun, thinking about the twenty-plus years behind and whatever may still come.

I hope afternoons like this grow more numerous in the years ahead.

A personal Meiji Restoration, a family reform and opening-up — in the end, both are so that I and the people close to me can live freer, richer and more complete lives.

To the bright future.
