Lately, while running, I keep coming back to a very plain question: why is it that if you do nothing, you slowly drift back to average?

In physics this is not a question at all. Everything on Earth's surface is pulled at \(9.8\ \mathrm{m/s^2}\). Apples fall, water flows downhill, heat dissipates. Nobody calls gravity a failure; it is simply the default state. Put that same default state into a life, though, and it acquires an uglier name: mediocrity.

## Gravity is the default option

What makes gravity so troublesome is that it requires no mistake on your part.

As long as you do not actively add thrust, it takes effect on its own. Skip writing for a day and your expression dulls; skip training for a week and your strength drops; go a month without real work and the project pushes you to its edge. None of this is punishment. It is just physics.

The gravity in a life has many sources: the content an algorithm hands you, the default pace of your peers, your family's expectation of "stability," the cash flow defined by rent and salary, and the identity you have spent years maintaining. Each one looks reasonable alone. Together they form a gravity well. You can live comfortably inside it, as long as you never look up.

For a stretch of time I lived very "normally": went to class on time, submitted assignments on time, prepared my résumé step by step. Nothing went wrong during that period. But looking back, a whole year produced almost nothing anyone else could see. It was not laziness. It was the absence of acceleration. Uniform motion inside a gravity field is simply falling.

## Orbit is not escape

There is an easy confusion here: reaching orbit is not the same as leaving.

Low Earth orbit takes roughly \(7.9\ \mathrm{km/s}\). Escape velocity is \(11.2\ \mathrm{km/s}\). The first is already absurdly fast, yet Earth still holds it firmly. The ratio between them is only \(\sqrt{2}\), about 1.41.

I find that number interesting. It says escape is not "twice the effort," nor a gap in talent, but roughly forty percent more speed — speed that must point in the same direction and keep accumulating. Many people's effort lands them exactly in orbit: good grades, respectable job, stable life, and it looks like flying. But they are still circling a planet; they just circle it beautifully.

An object in orbit is, in fact, always falling. It does not hit the ground because its horizontal speed is fast enough that the curve of its fall keeps pace with the curvature of the Earth. The people who look composed are the same: they have not escaped gravity. They have turned falling into circling.

So the question is not "am I trying hard enough," but "which direction am I accelerating in, and can I keep accelerating until I leave this system."

## Three lessons from the rocket equation

The Tsiolkovsky rocket equation is simple:

$$
\Delta v = v_e \ln\frac{m_0}{m_1}
$$

The velocity change you want is \(\Delta v\), \(v_e\) is the exhaust velocity (specific impulse), and \(m_0\) and \(m_1\) are the mass before and after ignition.

The cruelest part is the logarithm: to grow speed linearly, the mass ratio must grow exponentially. In practice, more than ninety percent of a rocket's mass is propellant, and what actually gets sent up is pitifully small. Three lessons follow.

**First, cutting mass is usually cheaper than adding fuel.** With the same engine, shedding dead weight buys more velocity change than loading a few more tonnes of propellant. The dead weight in a life is not body fat; it is everything that demands constant upkeep while producing no value: consumption with high maintenance cost, socializing with no informational gain, an identity that no longer fits, and habits kept only to look like you belong. Dropping them does not make you more hardworking. It makes you lighter.

**Second, raising specific impulse matters more than burning longer.** Specific impulse is thrust per unit of propellant; it is efficiency. In the same hour, using AI to read the material, draft the first version, and get the code running produces a completely different \(\Delta v\) from doing it by hand. Choosing high-leverage tools and directions is essentially swapping in a better engine. Many people refuse to change engines and only work overtime. That is patching efficiency with fuel, and you hit the mass-ratio wall soon enough.

**Third, staging.** A rocket does not fly with empty shells; the first stage burns out and is jettisoned. A person's "shell" is a stage-specific identity: the undergraduate shell, the first-job shell, the shell of a particular city, the shell of a particular relationship. They were useful once, maybe they even saved you, but in the next stage they are dead weight. Dropping a shell hurts, because the shell says "this is who I am." But if you do not drop it, you can only decelerate with it.

One launch detail is worth noting: a rocket does not climb straight up for long. It turns horizontal quickly. Gravity only eats velocity in the vertical direction, so the sooner you gain a horizontal component, the sooner you clear the well's mouth. For a person, that means do not grind forever on a single axis. GPA is vertical speed; work you can show, connections, and transferable skills are horizontal speed. Climb vertically for too long and gravity losses grow until orbit becomes impossible.

## Do not call structural gravity personal laziness

I need to pause here, because the "escape velocity" narrative very easily turns into another kind of motivational sermon: as if everyone standing still is simply not trying hard enough.

They are not. Gravity wells differ in depth. Some people are born on a mountain, some at the bottom of a well. Some have boosters; some must carry all the fuel themselves. Some have their well mouth sealed by policy or an industry cycle, and no amount of thrust gets them out. Acknowledging this is what keeps structural problems from being reduced to willpower.

Physics offers another answer too: escape velocity is always relative to a particular body. The Moon's escape velocity is only \(2.4\ \mathrm{km/s}\), less than a quarter of Earth's. Move to a weaker gravity field and the speed required is completely different. So "change city, change industry, change the people around you" is never mere avoidance. It is changing the reference frame. The same effort that only sustains a hover in a deep well may be enough to lift off in a shallow one.

I do not think everyone needs to escape. Earth's gravity is what keeps the atmosphere from dispersing and water flowing. Some gravity in a life is worth keeping: family, friends, responsibility, the work you want to do for a long time. What should be shed is the gravity that takes away your ability to choose — not all of it.

## Closing

The accounting I do for myself now is simple: each month, is there one thing that increased my \(\Delta v\)? Is there one thing I could jettison as dead weight? Is there a better engine I have not swapped in yet?

Most of the time I am still in orbit, and sometimes I lose altitude. That is normal. Gravity never stops.

What actually matters may be just this: light the engine once, in the right window, with the right direction — and know which orbit you want to reach. And decide whether you are willing to drop that first stage, already burned empty, in order to leave.
