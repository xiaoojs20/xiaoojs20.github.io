Mediocrity Has Gravity, and You Need Escape Velocity

Zhang Yiming, founder of ByteDance, wrote on Weibo early on: Mediocrity has gravity; you need escape velocity. In a 2015 interview with Chengdu Business Daily, he also said he had used “escaping the gravity of mediocrity” as a personal motto, connecting it to the idea of high standards gradually becoming a habit.

Mediocrity Is Not Stillness but Attraction

Externally, there is a gravitational field. In Newtonian mechanics, Earth’s gravitational field determines the acceleration of a freely falling body; in general relativity, free fall can be understood as motion along a geodesic in curved spacetime.

In everyday life, games offer frequent feedback and rewards, Hongguo short dramas keep lowering the cost of watching while stimulating the brain, and foods high in sugar, salt, and fat tend to have strong sensory appeal. Modern commercial society continuously optimizes these products so that immediate gratification becomes cheaper and cheaper to obtain.

Translated into a life, family background, social environment, industry structure, and financial pressure jointly shape a person’s opportunities and constraints. The lifestyle of people around us sets our reference point; the industry determines how abilities are rewarded; fixed expenses, debt, and responsibilities determine how much risk we can afford to take.

By contrast, learning, exercise, and creation usually require sustained investment and only pay off after a long delay. The asymmetry between immediate and delayed rewards forms a major external gravity in modern life.

Internally, there is mass and an existing state of motion. Mass measures inertia; changing velocity requires an impulse. If a body already has a downward velocity, reversing its motion first requires cancelling that downward momentum.

Over long evolution, humans developed physiological and psychological tendencies, including a preference for immediate rewards, avoidance of energy expenditure, and a search for security under uncertainty. Dopamine participates in motivation, reward prediction, and reinforcement learning, making rewarded behaviours more likely to be repeated. The human reward system did not evolve for the dense, low-cost stimuli of modern society, which is why we are so easily drawn to immediate rewards in the environment.

Behavioural inertia accumulated over a lifetime appears as long-formed habits, cognitive frames, vested interests, and path dependence. Repeated actions become automatic habits, and past successes can become obstacles to updating one’s understanding.

External gravity and internal inertia are not independent; they reinforce each other. The environment supplies stimuli, the internal reward system strengthens the behaviour, repetition turns it into a habit, and the habit then prompts the person to seek the same stimulus again. A person’s cognition, preferences, and behaviour are gradually shaped by the environment while, at the same time, they keep choosing environments that match their existing state. As this loop repeats, external constraints become internalized as behavioural inertia, and a lifestyle that was once easy to change can become difficult to change.

This may be the deeper reason mediocrity has gravity: the external environment keeps lowering the cost of staying put, while internal inertia keeps raising the cost of changing.

Countering Entropy Increase

The Direction of Entropy Increase

The statistical meaning of entropy increase is more precise than the everyday notion of disorder. The Boltzmann formula links entropy to the number of microstates corresponding to a macrostate:

$$ S=k_{\mathrm B}\ln\Omega $$

Here $\Omega$ is the number of microstates compatible with a given macrostate (under the description of equally probable microstates), and the Boltzmann constant converts its logarithm into entropy with physical dimensions.

Drop a drop of ink into water and it gradually spreads. It is not that physical law forbids the ink molecules from gathering again; it is simply that there are vastly more microscopic arrangements for the dispersed state than for the concentrated one. For macroscopic systems, moving from a low-probability state to a typical high-probability state is an overwhelming statistical trend.

Personal accumulation has a similar asymmetry: building a valuable state requires sustained investment, and maintaining it also carries a cost.

DimensionBuilding and accumulationNatural loss and decline
Physique and muscleMonths or years of regular trainingAfter training stops, muscle and strength can gradually be lost
Sleep and healthRegular schedule, enough sleep, and recoveryChronic sleep loss or irregular schedules can damage recovery and energy
Knowledge and skillsSystematic study, practice, and repeated useLong periods of disuse cause forgetting; technological change can erode value
Relationships and trustOngoing contact, repeated collaboration, keeping promisesNeglect can gradually thin relationships and trust
WealthContinuous saving, investing, and asset accumulationSpending, inflation, and investment losses can erode wealth
Work and reputationLong-term creation, value delivery, and external recognitionWork can become dated, and reputation and influence can fade

Rearranging my training, improving my physique, and beginning to take sleep and long-term health seriously gave me a more direct sense of this asymmetry. The body is not a system that can be overdrawn indefinitely and then repaired all at once after thirty.

This is not to say that every advantage is destined to decline. On the contrary, some knowledge, capital, and relationships can keep appreciating. But any functional structure has conditions for its maintenance, and building a structure is usually harder than destroying one.

There is also a more easily overlooked kind of decline: relative decline. Even if a person’s absolute ability stays the same, technology, industries, and other people keep improving, and the original ability can lose its scarcity. Standing still therefore also requires investment rather than simply stopping.

Decline does not require an active choice, while maintenance and growth usually require active investment.

Open Systems and Negentropy Flow

If decline is the natural trend, where does the resource for growth come from? Life offers a good reference. Prigogine’s theory of dissipative structures shows that an open system far from thermodynamic equilibrium can, under certain conditions, form and maintain organized structure through continuous exchange of matter and energy with its surroundings. Its entropy change can be split into internal entropy production and external entropy exchange:

$$ \frac{\mathrm dS}{\mathrm dt}=\frac{\mathrm d_iS}{\mathrm dt}+\frac{\mathrm d_eS}{\mathrm dt} $$$$ \frac{\mathrm d_iS}{\mathrm dt}\geq 0 $$

Internal entropy production is non-negative; the exchange term can be negative. If entropy exported exceeds internal production, the system’s entropy can fall.

Life maintains its non-equilibrium structure by taking in usable free energy, carrying out metabolism, and exporting heat and waste. Life does not violate the principle of entropy increase; it maintains and renews internal order through openness. Here negentropy flow refers to the negative entropy exchange corresponding to a net entropy export to the outside. Local entropy can decrease; if the system and its environment are taken together as an isolated whole, total entropy still does not decrease.

Teacher Jiu offers a perspective on personal growth that I strongly agree with: we tend to explain success through talent and effort while overlooking a third dimension — negentropy flow. Extending the dissipative-structure analogy to a life, it can be understood as high-quality information, experience, resources, and feedback from outside that help us renew our cognition and abilities.

DimensionMeaning in personal development
TalentEfficiency in understanding, processing, and transforming external input
EffortTime and energy invested in learning, practice, and creation
Negentropy flowRaw material for building and updating a personal system

The return on effort depends not only on how much is invested, but also on what information and problems it is invested in. Working hard for a long time in a low-quality information environment may be less effective than actively changing one’s information sources.

During the family and school years, our information environment is largely determined by existing conditions. After entering society, although we remain constrained by resources and opportunities, there is more room to actively choose information sources, peers, and practice environments. Teacher Jiu once shared the experience of a roommate working in security research: instead of using nearby colleagues’ progress as the main reference, the roommate kept tracking the problems that leading security researchers around the world were solving. This case does not prove that information sources determine success, but it made me realize that whom you follow, what you read, and what you use as a reference are themselves long-term decisions.

Of course, the closer one gets to the source, the more information there is, and the harder it becomes. Another of Teacher Jiu’s analogies is pretraining the brain: reading need not be treated like homework, requiring every paper to be read closely and every piece of information to be memorized. Long-term exposure to high-quality material helps build a holistic sense of a field’s problems, research directions, and technical changes; genuinely important questions deserve deeper pursuit, hands-on practice, and feedback to test understanding. This is not to say that browsing information equals learning, but that broad input should be combined with selective deep study.

Openness also does not mean endlessly increasing information input. A crystal is highly ordered but relatively simple; random noise has high information entropy but lacks functional organization. Valuable complexity lies in structure, division of labour, feedback, and adaptability. Collecting more papers, building a more elaborate knowledge base, or deploying more AI automation tasks does not necessarily mean a personal system has been upgraded. I hope these tools lower the cost of processing information, let knowledge settle into reusable understanding, and bring external feedback through practice, writing, and creation — rather than simply adding maintenance burden.

The quality of negentropy flow determines what we can obtain; our ability to process and organize it determines what we can turn it into. For me, what is worth maintaining over the long term is high-quality input, continuous learning and practice, and feedback mechanisms that keep correcting my understanding.

From Campus to Career: Rethinking Life Goals

As I write this, I am twenty-four, in the later part of my master’s programme, have finished job hunting, and am about to move from campus into a formal career. Over the past few years I have moved from electrical engineering toward the internet and algorithms, and have begun to think seriously about investing, health, writing, and travel. My clearer goals used to be getting into a good programme, finding internships, and landing a job. The question now is: after getting a good job, where exactly do I want to take my life?

Escape Velocity: Beyond Effort, You Need to Cross a Threshold

From Effort to Breakthrough

Entropy increase and open systems help us understand why existing structures need maintenance and how they can be renewed, but they cannot tell us how to leave a stable state that no longer fits our goals. Escape velocity offers another perspective.

In an ideal Newtonian gravitational model, if the central body’s mass is far greater than the object’s, the central body can be approximated as stationary. The object’s mechanical energy is the sum of kinetic energy and gravitational potential energy (with potential energy set to zero at infinity). If there is no further thrust, and no air resistance, collision, or interference from other bodies, escaping the gravitational binding requires non-negative total mechanical energy.

$$ E=\frac12 mv^2-\frac{GMm}{r}\geq 0 $$$$ v_{\mathrm e}=\sqrt{\frac{2GM}{r}} $$

Here $G$ is the gravitational constant, $M$ the mass of the central body, $m$ the mass of the object, $r$ the distance to the central body’s centre of mass, and $v$ the velocity relative to the central body. Ignoring Earth’s rotation and atmospheric drag, the ideal escape velocity at Earth’s surface is about $11.2\ \mathrm{km/s}$, while the circular orbital velocity in low Earth orbit is about $7.9\ \mathrm{km/s}$.

This suggests three kinds of effort. The first is compensatory effort: working to cover daily expenses, studying to avoid falling behind, exercising to offset sitting. The second is linear accumulation: salary, savings, and ability grow, but remain highly dependent on current labour. The third is structural breakthrough: existing knowledge, assets, work, technology, and reputation begin to generate new resources and change the growth mechanism of the next stage.

Breakthrough does not depend only on raising speed; it can also come from lowering constraints. Cutting unnecessary fixed expenses, entering industries and cities dense with opportunities, improving the transferability of skills, and using technology to reduce repetitive work are all changes to the constraints. Real life is even more flexible than the physical model: we can change the intensity of input, but also the direction, environment, and strategy.

This also means direction matters more than raw intensity. Working fourteen hours a day, if the value produced cannot settle into future ability and assets, may not bring one closer to autonomy than working eight hours while accumulating transferable skills and long-term work. A longer compounding window and lower switching costs in youth make trial and error with feedback especially valuable.

Finding a job is more like reaching first cosmic velocity: entering a new stable orbit, but not yet escaping gravity. What I need to watch next is whether my work is increasing abilities that are also valuable in the external market; whether it deepens my understanding of the link between technology and business; and if I left this company, how much of the past few years’ accumulation could I take with me. Compared with a single performance review, these questions are closer to the long-term growth rate I actually care about.

Accumulate Capital, Let Accumulation Compound

The measure of accumulation is what remains after one round of investment ends. Completing a project is current output; forming reusable methods, transferable abilities, and trustworthy collaborations is what can raise the return on the next round.

What is worth accumulating is not only financial capital, but also cognition, skills, health, social relationships, and personal influence. They can be converted into one another: knowledge raises professional income, income becomes assets, assets provide more autonomous time, time goes into learning and creation, and work and reputation bring new collaborative opportunities. The key to compounding is that today’s accumulation raises tomorrow’s productive capacity.

Excessive risk can force a sale at a low point and interrupt long-term compounding; skill accumulation without application and feedback can also lose value. What is worth pursuing is sustainable, effective growth, not short-term maximization of returns.

Accumulate Inward, Explore Outward

Lately I have been thinking about why I should deliberately expand my exposure to life. For a long time, my main environments were school, the lab, and internship companies. They gave me good conditions for learning and growth, but they also meant that the people, information, and opportunities I encountered were somewhat homogeneous. Even after moving to a new job, if daily life remains a commute between office and home, the boundaries of that environment can quickly re-form.

Even a capable person may fail to be matched with opportunities if they remain within a very small information and social circle. Suppose the probability that one effective external contact yields a valuable opportunity is $p$, and $N$ contacts are independent with the same success probability. Then the probability of getting at least one opportunity is:

$$ P=1-(1-p)^N $$

Real-world contacts involve correlation, quality differences, and resource constraints, so this model only illustrates the role of opportunity contact and cannot directly predict an individual’s probability of success. Learning and accumulation improve the quality of a single match; attending events, meeting people across fields, travelling, and writing publicly expand the range of contact. Both should happen at once. Exploring outward without internal accumulation makes it hard to take advantage of opportunities; accumulating inward without feedback and exposure can mean missing opportunities that would have been valuable.

What complex systems truly need is not ever more components, but diversity, modularity, redundancy, feedback, and adaptability: transferable abilities, diverse resource sources, local failures that do not drag down the whole, and the capacity to recognize errors and restructure oneself. For a person, this matters more than owning many accounts, tools, tasks, or contacts.

Countering natural loss is about not declining; effective accumulation is about progressing; crossing a threshold is about changing the mechanism of progress.

From Expanding Optionality to Continuous Evolution

A person’s professional income can grow while their ability to choose freely shrinks. In youth they want to start a business, travel, or write; later they find fixed costs too high to quit, their abilities too specialized to change fields, and all income coming from one institution, making risk unaffordable. Another path is also possible: as capital, skills, health, and social relationships accumulate, dependence on a single environment falls and the range of feasible future choices actually grows.

The difference between these paths is not how much one achieves now, but whether accumulation expands future possibilities. Buying a home, getting married, and holding stable employment are not problems in themselves; what matters is whether these commitments arise from one’s own will and fit the life one wants. An ordinary life is not the same as a mediocre one. Choosing a quiet, stable life actively is fundamentally different from being forced to stay in a situation one dislikes because of insufficient resources.

Seen this way, phased strategic imbalance makes sense. There is no need to invest evenly in every goal every year: some periods can focus on building career capital, others on assets or a creative system. As accumulation grows, what matters more is using the gained autonomous time for things one truly cares about.

But having options does not mean escaping constraints forever. Assets still need risk management, skills still need to adapt to technological change, and physical condition still needs maintenance. Environments and personal goals also change, and a development strategy that once worked may not keep working. Escape addresses how to break free of an existing binding; continuous evolution addresses how to retain optionality amid change. This requires continually obtaining external resources, accepting feedback, and adjusting one’s abilities and resource structure to new conditions.

If I look only at my calendar, I can easily fit research, work, investing, training, travel, socializing, and writing into it and feel that I am growing rapidly. But limited time means they cannot all expand without bound. What I should watch instead is whether these investments leave long-term value, reduce dependence on a single environment, and make me more able to cope with change.

Dimension I care aboutReal accumulationCounterexample to watch
CareerTransferable skills, business understanding, independent problem solvingOnly adding hours, relying on internal tools or rank
WealthNet assets, cash buffer, sustainable expense coverageSpending rising with income, excessive leverage
HealthTraining continuity, sleep and recovery, long-term physical stateTrading future health for short-term output
OpennessCross-field exchange, real relationships, effective attempts in new settingsAdding events and contacts without purpose
CreationIndependent work, distilled knowledge, external feedbackOnly collecting information or optimizing tools and processes

If relative ranking becomes the only goal, there is always a new gravitational field: after ten million comes a hundred million, and after one promotion comes a higher position. A person can look successful while remaining entirely governed by competition and external evaluation. Similarly, continuous evolution does not mean endlessly increasing life’s complexity. Sometimes trimming unnecessary commitments, preserving energy, and establishing simple, effective rules improve adaptability more.

We cannot stop time, nor fully avoid ageing, forgetting, and loss; but within a finite life we can keep building order, accumulating resources, creating value, and expanding future possibilities. The measure of escape is not how high a position was reached in how short a time, but whether past accumulation has given me more right to decide my next step.

Mediocrity has gravity, and decline has entropy increase. Breakthrough requires escape velocity; sustained development requires the capacity to keep evolving.
